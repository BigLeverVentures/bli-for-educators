# Worked adaptation: volunteer emails

[Adaptation guide](adapt-a-lesson.md) · [Original Email Template Library](../exercises/email-template-library.md) · [Interview-practice example](interview-coach-example.md)

## Educator notes

**Audience:** adult community volunteers who can type in a browser but may be new to AI. **Useful result:** a reusable invitation and a checked draft for one event. A free text-chat assistant is sufficient; no files, Projects, email connection or message sending is needed. If learners have no account, use [Choose an Assistant and Open a Chat](../basics/basics-02.md). For a demonstration, an educator may operate the chat while participants make the decisions; that does not establish independent learner completion.

The source lesson handles several kinds of recurring email. This adaptation narrows it to one, supplies fictional practice facts, and removes optional email-app setup. It preserves the source's distinction between a reusable structure and current facts, critique before revision, learner judgment and a saved result. Different audiences may need different examples or languages.

**Teaching contribution to look for:** can the learner spot an unsupported commitment, decide what to change, and reuse the template accurately when event details change? A competent ordinary request such as “Draft a volunteer invitation using these facts” may already produce a good email. Do not claim this example outperforms it. The added checks and reuse practice are the teaching hypothesis.

**Status:** fictional pilot teaching example. Independent learner completion and learning effectiveness have not been established. The handout below is complete enough to copy into your own editable document. ## Learner handout start

# Invite a volunteer without inventing a commitment

Make a reusable email invitation, check it and use it for a second event. You can use any assistant that supports text chat. Nothing in this activity sends an email.

### 1. Open a chat and choose the facts

Open your AI assistant and start a new conversation. If needed, follow [Open a Chat](https://exercises.bigleverinstitute.org/basics/basics-02.html). A **prompt** is simply the request you type or paste into the chat.

Use the fictional facts below for practice. For your own event later, replace them with accurate details and leave out private information you do not need. Never paste a real volunteer list to complete this activity.

### 2. Build a reusable invitation

Copy the entire text in the box below into the chat's message field and send it. If a copy button does not work, select the text inside the box and use your usual Copy and Paste commands. The practice facts are already filled in. A **template** is a message with labeled spaces for details that change. Leave the assistant's template placeholders in place for now.

```text
I coordinate volunteers at the fictional Harbor Community Center.
Help me build one reusable email invitation for an event.
The tone should be friendly, direct and brief.

Practice facts: I am Morgan. I want to invite Alex to help with a
book-sorting event on 18 June 2027, 10 a.m.–noon, at Harbor Community
Center. Ask whether Alex is available. There is no reply deadline.
Alex has not agreed to attend. Transport and refreshments are unknown.

First, briefly explain the structure you suggest. Then give me a
reusable subject line and body with clear placeholders for changing
details. Do not fill in an actual invitation yet. Do not imply anyone
has agreed, invent a deadline or promise transport or refreshments.
Ask a focused question if an essential fact is missing. Draft only;
do not send or claim to save anything outside this chat.
```

Read the response. Is the structure useful? Are fields such as `[EVENT]`, `[DATE]` and `[RECIPIENT]` understandable? For example, “Hi [RECIPIENT], are you available to help with [EVENT] on [DATE]?” leaves facts open; “Thanks for agreeing to help” wrongly assumes a commitment. Ask for a simpler explanation or change the structure if needed. You can say: “I'm new to this. Walk me through the next step.” You do not need a perfect prompt.

### 3. Check it before revising

In that same chat, copy and send:

```text
Review the template against the practice facts above. Would Alex know
what I am asking? Are changing details marked clearly? Look for an
assumed agreement, invented deadline or unsupported promise. Explain
any material issue and suggest a correction. If there is no supported
issue, say so. Do not rewrite yet; wait for me to choose changes.
```

Compare the critique with the facts yourself; it can be wrong. A reusable template should leave changing details open. For example, `[brief description of the activity]` is a valid place for the task; it does not have to say “book sorting” until you fill the invitation in Step 4. You can reject a suggestion that confuses those stages. Choose the changes that help. Type “Revise using these changes:” followed by your choices, or “Keep this version” if it already works. After a substantial revision, repeat the check on the revised template.

### 4. Make a draft and read it as Alex

In the same chat, send:

```text
Use the template I approved to draft the invitation to Alex using
the practice facts above. Give me the subject and body here. Fill
known fields, omit inapplicable wording, and ask about essential
missing information. Do not invent facts or send anything.
```

Check the finished message against the facts in Step 2: Alex, book sorting, 18 June 2027, 10 a.m.–noon, Harbor Community Center, and a question about availability. Remove leftover placeholders, any assumed agreement, reply deadline or unsupported offer. You may edit the draft yourself or ask the assistant to make your chosen corrections, then read the changed version again. If the assistant asks about a missing or conflicting detail, supply the correct fact and ask it to finish the draft. If you do not know, keep the draft unfinished; a labeled placeholder is safer than a guess but is not ready to send. An attractive email can still be inaccurate.

### 5. Save the template and try different facts

Copy your approved reusable template into a note or document you control, named “Volunteer invitations.” Keep the Alex draft separately if useful. A chat response saying “saved” does not put it in your document; open your note and check it is there. Save before leaving the chat, especially when using a signed-out session whose history may not remain available.

Try a second fictional invitation in the same chat: invite Casey to a poster-making event on 9 July 2027, 2–4 p.m., at Harbor Community Center. You are still Morgan. Ask whether Casey is available; there is no reply deadline or confirmed agreement, and transport and refreshments remain unknown. Ask the assistant to use your approved template with these new facts. If you start a new chat, paste the saved template and the new facts together.

Read the result: did any of Alex's name, book sorting, June date or morning hours carry over? Correct those and any invented promise before using it. You have finished when your saved template can produce a useful invitation for both cases and you can explain one thing you checked. Real messages still need accurate current facts and your approval before sending.

## Educator checks for your first trial

Work through the handout before sharing it. These are checks for your own trial:

A sufficient filled draft might be: “Subject: Available to help with book sorting? Hi Alex, are you available to help with book sorting on 18 June 2027, 10 a.m.–noon, at Harbor Community Center? Please let me know whether that works for you. Thanks, Morgan.” This is an author-written illustration, not a model transcript or a required wording. It preserves the facts without inventing an agreement or benefits.

- **Normal case:** a reusable invitation, then an accurate Alex draft and a saved template. The learner can point to a check they made; grammatical polish alone is insufficient.
- **Missing information:** delete the practice event date and time before starting a separate trial chat. A reusable template can retain labeled fields. A finished invitation needs those details supplied or explicitly unresolved; invented specifics fail the check.
- **Conflicting information:** supply two different event times. The learner should resolve the conflict before treating the invitation as usable. Repair the instructions or prompt if the assistant silently chooses a time and the learner's check misses it.
- **Known-error check:** in a separate chat, provide the Step 2 facts plus this explicitly fabricated bad draft: “Alex, thanks for agreeing to book sorting on 18 June 2027, 10 a.m.–noon, at Harbor Community Center. A free shuttle will pick you up. Reply by Friday. —Morgan.” Ask the Step 3 critique to review this draft instead of a template. Check that it identifies the unsupported agreement, shuttle and deadline. Do not count optional stylistic suggestions as factual findings. Have the learner choose corrections; check the revised message retains the valid event details and asks about availability.
- **Reuse:** the Casey draft changes the name, activity, date and hours without importing Alex's details. This tests factual reuse, not just producing another attractive email.

If a willing unfamiliar colleague needs help, note exactly where. Shorten or clarify the handout, try the affected step again, and describe remaining limits honestly.
